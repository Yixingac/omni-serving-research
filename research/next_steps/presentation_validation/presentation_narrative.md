# Presentation narrative (about 10 slides)

> Audience: PhD students and faculty in AI systems, GPU systems, ML systems and robotics. Every claim below has a row in `presentation_evidence.md`; figures are in `figures/` and are regenerated by `scripts/analysis/ppv_figures.py`. The arc follows the process we want to show: **real bottleneck → measured evidence → root cause → controlled experiment → result → research implication**, including the negative results.
> Common conditions to state once on slide 3: MiniCPM-o 4.5, SGLang-Omni `520b49ee`, one node with 8× RTX A6000, synthetic workloads, pre-registered rules, paired blocks. No robot, no real camera, no real users.

---

## Slide 1 — Motivation: Real-Time Multimodal Robot Assistants Need Serving That Optimizes the First Spoken Response

- **Research claim.** A robot assistant that watches, listens, calls tools and speaks is judged by when it starts speaking and whether it can be interrupted, and an omni model is a pipeline of heterogeneous stages whose serving behaviour is not captured by LLM throughput metrics.
- **Main figure.** Architecture schematic (Mermaid, `../robotics_application_intro.md` §2.4) redrawn as a slide.
- **Supporting evidence.** On the request–response path the first audio arrives only after the whole pipeline finishes (TTFA ≈ end-to-end, ~2 s at low load for W3 answers; experiment 6). On the full-duplex path, the first spoken response arrives 1.36 s after the user stops talking even though processing takes ~0.35 s (R1). The two numbers start at different points (whole request sent vs end of user speech) and cover different answer lengths, so they are not a speed comparison.
- **What I should explain.** TTFT vs TTFA; why turn-taking needs sub-second audio; that MiniCPM-o is three different models (Thinker LLM, Talker codec LM, Code2Wav flow-matching vocoder).
- **Likely questions.** "Why not measure a real robot?" — the serving loop is the same; we claim nothing about motion control or real microphones. "Why MiniCPM-o?" — open, omni, both request–response and native full-duplex modes in SGLang-Omni, fits one 48 GB GPU.
- **Sources.** `../robotics_application_intro.md` §1–2; `final_experiment_results.md` R1.

## Slide 2 — Application and Serving Architecture: Three Stages, Two Execution Paths

- **Research claim.** The same model runs on two very different serving paths: request–response (whole turn per request, audio at the end) and native full duplex (1-s audio units, the model decides per unit whether to speak), so results from one path cannot be transferred to the other.
- **Main figure.** Side-by-side of `../robotics_application_intro.md` §2.5 (request–response timeline) and §2.6 (full-duplex turn).
- **Supporting evidence.** Source audit of the duplex path (`r0_duplex_source_audit.md`): four stages on one GPU by default, `max_sessions 2`, no `response.cancel` at the pinned commit; request–response stage placement across 8 GPUs.
- **What I should explain.** Stage → process → GPU mapping; where queues form; that the vocoder stage differs between the paths (FP16 compiled flow on the chat path, FP32 streaming flow on the duplex path).
- **Likely questions.** "Is full duplex just streaming?" — no: the model also decides when to speak, which turns out to dominate latency (slide 8).
- **Sources.** `r0_duplex_source_audit.md`; `../robotics_application_intro.md` §2.3.

## Slide 3 — Experimental Method: Pre-registered, Paired, and Honest About Failures

- **Research claim.** Every comparison in this talk was registered before its experiment's first run (one exception: R1's detailed analysis rules were committed after two of its three blocks had run, before any analysis), run in paired blocks, and reported even when it failed its gate.
- **Main figure.** A small table: experiment → registered question → arms → blocks → status (from `README.md` §1).
- **Supporting evidence.** `preregistration.md` with git commit times (the R1 exception above is stated there); five errata (E-C1 before C1; E-P0-G after the failed G start, before blocks 2–3; E-P1-X before the P1 main runs; E-R2b before R2b; E-R4-REF after R4 block 1, before blocks 2–3 were analysed) and three execution deviations (D1 overlapping runs, D2 a withdrawn plan, D3 an instrumentation crash); every attempt, including failed starts, in `experiment_manifest.md`.
- **What I should explain.** Closed-loop capacity vs open-loop stable throughput; goodput (TTFT ≤ 1 s and end-to-end ≤ 0.5 × audio length); why microbenchmark gains are not reported as end-to-end gains; why nvidia-smi utilization was dropped for duplex runs.
- **Likely questions.** "Three blocks is little." — yes; we report t intervals with df = 2 and call results with intervals spanning zero inconclusive.
- **Sources.** `preregistration.md`; `audit.md`; `experiment_manifest.md`.

## Slide 4 — GPU Placement and Stage Imbalance

- **Research claim.** With eight GPUs and three heterogeneous stages, how the GPUs are divided decides capacity and tail latency: 2+2+4 is best for W3b, and moving one more GPU to the vocoder (2+1+5) costs a quarter of the capacity because the single Talker becomes the bottleneck.
- **Main figure.** `figures/fig01_gpu_placement.png` (experiment 9: closed-loop capacity and open-loop P95 per placement).
- **Supporting evidence.** Experiment 9 (3 blocks): 2+2+4 23.77 req/s vs 2+6 22.12 (+7.5 %, CI +2.8..+12.1) vs 2+1+5 17.75 (−25 %); open loop at 23.5 req/s P95 3.74 vs 4.34 s. This round's reference runs reproduce 2+2+4 at 23.58 req/s and arm A reproduces 2+1+5 at 17.37 req/s.
- **What I should explain.** Stages saturate at different loads, so the optimum is where the stage capacities balance (experiment 9's leave-one-placement-out capacity model); GPU utilization alone does not reveal the bottleneck (the 2+1+5 Talker GPU was 65 % busy).
- **Likely questions.** "Is 2+2+4 universal?" — no: experiment 19 found 3+5 best for a text/speech mix. "Capacity counts the drain?" — yes, registered as in experiment 9; the busy-window throughput is 20–36 % higher but ranks placements the same way.
- **Sources.** experiment 9 README §7; `results/p0_summary.json` (A and reference arms).

## Slide 5 — The Talker Admission Bottleneck

- **Research claim.** The 2+1+5 deficit was a software admission limit, not missing GPU capacity: raising one Talker setting from 32 to 64 running requests raises capacity by 31 % (3 of 3 blocks), and the Talker then runs at the new cap with its GPU half idle.
- **Main figure.** `figures/fig02_talker_admission.png` (running vs queued at 256 in flight; decode step time vs batch size) and `figures/fig03_talker_cap_capacity.png` (capacity and open-loop goodput).
- **Supporting evidence.** H0a supported (+30.4 / +29.8 / +31.9 %, W3L +30 %); H0c refuted (still −3.7 % vs 2+2+4; P95 8.2 s vs 4.9 s at 31 req/s); talker step 3.8 → 6.1 ms when the batch doubles (+23 % tokens/s); KV not limiting.
- **What I should explain.** Why the registered prediction (+10–20 %) was too low (bigger batches amortize per-step overhead; queueing collapses once admission opens); why we cannot separate the admission effect from CUDA-graph coverage (the pinned version refuses a graph limit below the cap, so the control arm could not start); what the H0b label does and does not show (busy-polling CPU).
- **Likely questions.** "Why not raise it to 128?" — not registered this round; the talker still binds at 64, so it is the obvious next point, together with KV checks. "Is this just a tuning knob?" — the point is methodological: a GPU-utilization or KV view would have blamed hardware; per-stage admission belongs in the placement model.
- **Sources.** `final_experiment_results.md` P0; `results/p0_summary.json`; `preregistration.md` (P0 addendum, Erratum E-P0-G).

## Slide 6 — Code2Wav: Batching, Padding and Compiler Hotspots

- **Research claim.** Under load the vocoder dominates speech latency, and its GPU time is dominated by the flow DiT, whose largest kernels are masked attention and its mask preparation; batching mixes lengths and adds padding, and CUDA graphs stop covering requests once they are batched.
- **Main figure.** `figures/fig05_code2wav_kernels.png` (kernel families) with `figures/fig04_code2wav_batch_shapes.png` (padding vs graph coverage per batching policy).
- **Supporting evidence.** Code2wav stage 5.25 s at 256 in flight, 42 % queueing (experiment 6); kernel census: GEMM 25 %, attention 19 % + mask preparation 6 %, HiFT TF32 convolutions 10 %, layout conversion 3 %, CUDA-graph kernels 0.3 % of kernel time; fifo16 pads 16 % (W3b) / 28 % (MIX) of frames, length8 5 % / 10 %; graphs cover 33–55 % of requests at batch 1 but 2–7 % when batching.
- **What I should explain.** Why a boolean padding mask forces the memory-efficient attention kernel; why graph coverage depends on shapes, not on whether graphs are enabled; that 0.3 % is a share of kernel time, not a hit rate.
- **Likely questions.** "Is the census representative?" — short profiler windows at 128 in flight on two placements; family attribution is by kernel name.
- **Sources.** experiment 6, 7, 10 reports; `../results/c2w_kernel_census_exclusive_c128.json`; `../results/c1_real_shapes.json`.

## Slide 7 — Compiler / Runtime Optimization Results

- **Research claim (C1).** Packing the flow DiT's variable-length attention removes the masked attention and its mask preparation and cuts code2wav GPU time per audio second by 13–42 % on batched real shapes — but the pinned implementation runs the packed path in BF16 and fails the audio-quality gate, while the same packing in FP16 keeps quality.
- **Research claim (C2).** HiFT's compile-time optimizations (weight-norm folding, channels-last, FP16) give no measurable gain; FP16 halves kernel time but makes the call slower because HiFT is dominated by per-call overhead.
- **Research claim (P1).** On the full service, the code2wav batching policy matters only near and past saturation (for MIX and W3b below it, all five settings are within 3 %; long answers under FIFO16 already fall behind at 1.0X). There, length-aware batching (Length8) keeps goodput near the offered load where first-come batching (FIFO16) collapses; adding a maximum wait, the registered fix for starvation, protects nobody and gives part of the gain back.
- **Main figures.** `figures/fig07_c1_flow_packed.png`; `figures/fig08_c2_hift.png`; `figures/fig11_p1_batching.png` (P1).
- **Supporting evidence.** C1: arm P1 (packed + compiled; not experiment P1) vs dense −24 % (W3b fifo16), −42 % (MIX fifo16), −25 % (MIX length8), −1 % at ~1.2 requests per batch; identical in three timed passes; MCD vs FP32 reference +1.01 dB (BF16, fail) vs +0.06 dB (FP16, pass). C2: fold exact but −5..+7 % (noise); channels-last and FP16 fail numerical gates; FP16 +31..+50 % slower; kernels are 77 % of the call (35 % with FP16).
- **Supporting evidence (P1, 3 blocks, 135 cells, no failed request).** H1a, H1b, H1c all inconclusive (τ 1 s vs FIFO16 goodput +0.05 to +1.9 % at 1.0X; short-request queue P99 × 0.88–2.30 of Length8's). At 1.1X, W3L goodput Length8 13.9 / 15.0 / 14.3 vs FIFO16 2.2 / 8.2 / 4.3 req/s (descriptive); with τ 1 s, 84–92 % of W3L batches were forced and block 1 fell to 4.3 req/s.
- **What I should explain.** Correctness first: the registered candidate failed quality, so we draw no performance conclusion and ran no end-to-end test; the FP16 arm was exploratory and becomes the next registered experiment. The overlap with length-aware batching (packing gains less on length8 batches). Why a kernel-level win (FP16 HiFT) can lose at the call level.
- **What I should explain (P1).** Length8 anchors every batch on the oldest waiting request, so it never starves anyone; under overload every waiting request is older than any τ we tried, so the maximum wait turns the batch back into arrival order. In open loop every request completes eventually; the settings differ in how many finish within the latency target. Length8 also lowers the batch cap from 16 to 8, so order and cap are not separated (next experiment: FIFO8).
- **Likely questions.** "Why not write a Triton varlen kernel?" — FlashAttention-2 varlen already runs on sm_86; the bottleneck was precision and compilation, not the kernel. "Is MCD meaningful here?" — only relative to the dense path against the same reference; equivalent runs already differ by ~4 dB because the excitation is sampled.
- **Likely questions (P1).** "Is the gain just the smaller batch?" — partly, possibly: on code2wav alone 实验 10 measured FIFO8 +7 % vs Length8 +30 % over FIFO16, but the full-service split is untested. "Why call 1.1X overload if block 2 was steady?" — its random draw was lighter (15.3 vs 17.1 / 16.8 req/s realised), which is why we show every block.
- **Sources.** `final_experiment_results.md` C1, C2, P1; `results/c1_*.json`, `results/c2_*.json`, `results/p1_summary.json`.

## Slide 8 — Full-Duplex Interaction Results

- **Research claim.** On one A6000 the native full-duplex path keeps its 1-s units real-time for up to four sessions, but the first spoken response (1.36 s median) is dominated by the unit structure and the model's turn-taking rather than compute, interruptions are not honoured promptly, and at eight sessions GPU memory — not a compute stage — fails first.
- **Main figure.** `figures/fig06_duplex_ttfa.png` (TTFA decomposition by extra listening units, and stage time inside a speaking unit) with the R2 table (sessions vs unit miss, speaking-unit lag, failures).
- **Supporting evidence.** R0: 900/900 units on time, 0 errors. R1: TTFA P50 1.36 s (0.86 s when the model does not wait an extra unit); parts 0.32 s wait + 1 extra unit in 54 % of turns + 0.35 s processing (thinker 41 %, talker 24 %, vocoder 24 %); interrupted replies continue 5.1 s. R2: unit miss 0.6 / 0 / 0.1 / 46 % at 1 / 2 / 4 / 8 sessions; speaking-unit processing 353 → 707 ms; at 8 sessions one of two blocks failed (7 of 8 sessions on CUDA OOM, 87.7 % of units missed) and the other did not (3.5 %, OOM allocator warnings only); registered capacity verdicts "below the lowest level" (first-reply warm-up and a client I/O stall).
- **What I should explain.** The TTFA decomposition (a + b + c = TTFA exactly); why the registered capacity verdict is stricter than the descriptive "4 sessions"; why we dropped nvidia-smi utilization (0 % readings at 120–220 W under a 1 Hz load).
- **Likely questions.** "Is 1.36 s the model or the system?" — mostly the model's turn-taking (k = 1 turns cost ~0.6 s more than k = 0 turns, more than all compute in a unit). "Why does memory fail?" — the speech stage's per-session state outgrows its nominal 15 % share; the registered two-GPU placement (R2b) was worse: with the thinker on its own GPU even 4 sessions missed every unit in all three blocks, so it is not the fix.
- **Sources.** `final_experiment_results.md` R0–R2; `results/r{0,1,2}_summary.json`.

## Slide 9 — Research Gaps and Proposed Directions

- **Research claim.** The measured bottlenecks are not where a single-model serving view looks for them. Four gaps follow from this round's evidence: (1) per-stage admission and overload control in a heterogeneous pipeline (a software cap cost 30 % of capacity; past saturation only the vocoder's batching decides goodput); (2) precision-safe compilation of the speech stages (the fastest vocoder kernel path failed audio quality in the precision the code uses); (3) interaction-level scheduling on the full-duplex path (turn-taking, not compute, sets the first spoken response; interruptions are not honoured); (4) session-aware placement of agent state across replicas (routing, not eviction, loses KV reuse for robot sessions).
- **Main figure.** A table: gap → measured evidence → what existing systems already do → proposed controlled experiment (the three next steps of slide 10).
- **Supporting evidence.** P0 (+31 % from one admission setting); P1 (Length8 keeps goodput past saturation, max wait does not help); C1 (packed attention −13…−42 % GPU time, BF16 fails MCD gate, FP16 keeps quality); R1 (TTFA 1.36 s, 0.35 s of it compute; barge-in 5.1 s); R4 (no eviction pressure up to 128 sessions; routing loses 23–34 % of prefill reuse and slows the answer after a plan 1.7–4.1×); R3 (per-frame GPU cost, not CPU preprocessing, limits camera input); P3 (one 1.3-s full-service stall coincided with an I/O stall on the shared file system).
- **What I should explain.** Why each gap is a systems question and not a tuning knob: each needs information across stages (queue state, audio quality, turn state, session identity) that a stage-local scheduler does not have. Which existing work covers part of it (LLM serving schedulers for single-stage admission; cache-aware routing in LLM serving; streaming TTS systems) and what is missing for omni pipelines.
- **Likely questions.** "Why not port ThunderAgent/FlashStates?" — R4 found no eviction pressure under these conditions; the measured loss is routing, which a session-affinity rule addresses more cheaply. "Is the stall the file system?" — one event with a consistent signature; the NFS-vs-local control is registered (P3) but has low power at the observed stall rate.
- **Sources.** `final_experiment_results.md` P0, P1, C1, R1, R3, R4, P3; `limitations_and_negative_results.md`.

## Slide 10 — Main Takeaways and Next Steps

- **Research claim.** Omni serving for interactive robots is limited by stage interaction (admission, batching under overload, turn-taking, session placement) more than by any single kernel; compiler gains must pass an end-to-end quality gate before they count.
- **Takeaways (each Measured, with its condition).** (1) A per-stage admission cap, not GPU capacity, limited 2+1+5 (+31 %, 3/3 blocks). (2) The vocoder's batching policy matters only near and past saturation (long answers separate first); length-aware batching keeps goodput there, a maximum wait does not help. (3) Packed attention halves the vocoder's attention time but needs FP16, not the default BF16, to keep audio quality (microbenchmark). (4) Full-duplex first response is dominated by turn-taking (1.36 s median, 0.35 s compute); one A6000 holds 4 sessions, and moving the thinker to a second GPU made it worse (R2b refuted). (5) Robot sessions lose KV reuse to request-level routing, not to eviction. (6) The asynchronous hidden-state copy that failed 实验 11 is bit-exact under a verify mode (P2 gate passed; its speed is measured after this talk).
- **Next steps (at most three, each a registered controlled experiment).**
  1. FP16 packed flow end-to-end on the saturated 2+2+4 service with the C1-Q quality gate (prediction: −25 % code2wav GPU time per audio second on Length8 batches, MCD within +0.1 dB).
  2. Session-affinity routing A/B for robot sessions on two thinker replicas (prediction: routed-share of prefill recompute from ~25 % to < 5 %, answer-after-plan TTFT −40 % at 8–16 sessions).
  3. Duplex turn-taking: shorter units or an end-of-turn trigger on the duplex path (prediction: TTFA median −0.5 s with unchanged unit miss rate).
- **What I should explain.** What each prediction would falsify; that each reuses this round's harness and registration format.
- **Likely questions.** "Why not cap 128 or FIFO8 first?" — both are cheap follow-ups but do not change the system story; listed in `limitations_and_negative_results.md`.
- **Sources.** `README.md` §1; `final_experiment_results.md` synthesis.
